What Would It Mean for Machines to Do Research?

A Conceptual Exploration Drawing on Accounts of Human Inquiry

Shiro Takagi


This is the full text of the book in HTML, one page per chapter. It is also available as a PDF (English, Japanese) and on Zenodo (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23851153).

Abstract

What would it mean for machines to do research, and what might change in research when machines do it? Reports of results produced by machines now arrive in quick succession, and with them disputes over what exactly was solved, to whom a result is new, and which part of the work the machine did. This book is a conceptual exploration of these questions. It starts from a thin working characterization: relative to a society S, research is an attempt to bring about epistemic achievements new to S. This lets the one doing research be a machine and lets S be human, mixed, or artificial. The book then draws on accounts of knowledge, understanding, inquiry, and the social organization of research that were developed with human researchers in mind, and follows, account by account, what each implies when the researcher is a machine. It asks when a machine's output becomes knowledge or understanding, and for whom; how inquiry proceeds before there is an answer, from making a question researchable to locating a failure and revising standards; when a configuration can be credited with conducting research and with autonomy, and whose knowledge results; and whether the allocation of inquiry, criticism and certification, and authority over decisions can move inside an artificial configuration. Two things come into view. The claim that a machine is doing research divides into claims that can hold or fail separately. And much that was settled without being decided in human research, because researchers were persons divided among many, becomes something that can be decided: set by builders, shaped by the system itself, or lost without notice. Who holds a belief, how independent critics are, who answers for an error, and against what a revision of standards is measured are examples. The book does not settle these questions; it tries to name what research has rested on, so that they can be asked.

  • The text of this book was written mainly by the AI agents Claude (Anthropic) and Codex (OpenAI), working under the author's direction.
  • Historical and contemporary cases are used to bring the argument into contact with research practice, not as a representative sample of all research practice.
  • The book does not claim that any single definition of research supplies an objective to be optimized in building artificial systems that do research.
